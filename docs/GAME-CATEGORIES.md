# The Sara Show — party board specification

**Recommendation: Famous Saras, The Birthday Girl, What's That Country?, The Roaring Twenties, The Sara Archives, and Party Science.** Six categories, five questions each, 100–500 points. Keep Bad Movie Plots as the prepared substitute for Party Science.

This is the proposed **party content**, not a change to the running game. The current public pack is a rehearsal pack: 22 playable questions and eight disabled personal/mashup drafts. Its job is to test the board, timer, scoring, memes and television setup. It should not become the final birthday quiz by default.

## Why these six

| Board label | Norwegian label | What the team actually does | Why it earns a column |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Famous Saras | Kjente Saraer | Names a real Sara/Sarah or a fictional Sarah from clues. | Preserves the “Famous Philips” format. Mix acting, music, sport and a fictional character. |
| The Birthday Girl | Bursdagsbarnet | Spots the celebrity underneath a Sara face mashup. | The visual comic centrepiece; no knowledge of Sara's private life required. |
| What's That Country? | Hvilket land? | Recognises outlines, landmarks and map clues. | Gives the whole room an accessible way into the game. |
| The Roaring Twenties | De glade 20-årene | Solves short clues about the actual period and Gatsby. | Makes the party theme part of the game, with real answers. |
| The Sara Archives | Sara-arkivet | Works out the real story behind photos, quotes and memories. | Makes this Sara's birthday game rather than a generic pub quiz. |
| Party Science | Festvitenskap | Explains familiar things: fizz, ice, sound and a spinning ball. | Adds a different kind of thinking and connects the room, the drinks and Sara's table-tennis days. |

**Choose Sara Archives plus Party Science for the last two columns.** The first two categories already give film/TV and celebrity fans plenty to do; another full film category would give that knowledge too much weight. Science lets a different teammate take the lead. It should be observation and reasoning, with no medical alcohol trivia or school-exam calculations.

Bad Movie Plots is the simplest substitute if the guest group strongly prefers films. The existing five clues can be retained and polished. If personal stories are not ready, play a smaller Sara Archives column rather than inventing memories or filling it with five versions of “How old is Sara?”

## Common progression

| Value | Intended feel | Question design |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | Most teams can get started. | Familiar subject, strong visual/context clue, one short answer. |
| 200 | A moment of recognition. | One useful clue removed; still broadly familiar. |
| 300 | Teammates should confer. | Two clues to connect, or a credible three-option question. |
| 400 | A rewarding deduction. | Less context or a second reasoning step; no microscopic details. |
| 500 | A proper final challenge. | A fair combination of clues, a short matching/order task, or a hard but recognisable picture. |

Points are a rehearsal hypothesis, not a promise of universal difficulty. Try the pictures on two people who will not play, and reorder them by what they actually find difficult. More points must not mean smaller text, a less visible face, a mistranslated pun or a fact only one guest could possibly know.

## 1. Famous Saras

This round is about the **name**. The answer is a Sara/Sarah, not whichever celebrity happens to be merged with her. Adding a musician and a Swedish athlete gives it more range than five television questions.

| Value | Format and specific proposed clue | Accepted answer | Preparation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | Name clue: “Carrie Bradshaw in Sex and the City. Which Sarah?” | Sarah Jessica Parker; accept “Sarah Jessica.” | Ready text; a permitted portrait can be added. |
| 200 | Role clue: “She was Buffy on television. Name the Sarah.” | Sarah Michelle Gellar. | Ready text; distinguish the TV series from the earlier film. |
| 300 | Two song-title cards: **Love Song** and **Brave**. “Which Sara recorded these?” | Sara Bareilles; sensible pronunciation accepted. | New text/graphic clue; no music playback needed. |
| 400 | Fictional identity: “Linda Hamilton plays her. The Terminator is looking for her. Which Sarah?” | Sarah Connor. | Reuse the verified starter answer, with a more theatrical presentation. |
| 500 | Sport clue with a Swedish flag: “This Sarah won the women's 50 m and 100 m freestyle at Paris 2024. Surname?” | Sjöström; Sjostrom/Sjoestroem accepted. | New sourced clue; use a permitted competition portrait if helpful. |

The song association is confirmed by [Sara Bareilles' official site](https://sarabmusic.com/pages/goodgriefdoc). The two Paris gold medals are recorded in the [Swedish Olympic Committee's 2024 report](https://sok.se/download/18.6fb190381967fbdc1502179/1745962837051/2024_SOKVerksamhetsberattelse.pdf). Existing cast sources for Parker, Gellar and Connor are in [GAME-CONTENT.md](GAME-CONTENT.md).

Sarah Paulson and Sarah Lund remain good swaps if the group is more familiar with American Crime Story or Nordic crime television than music or swimming. The host only needs to flag unfamiliar references; nobody needs to provide personal Sara facts for this round.

## 2. The Birthday Girl

Every prompt should be the same clear instruction: **“Sara has taken over. Who is the original celebrity?”** The answer reveal shows the original reference next to the Sara version so the room can enjoy the comparison. The following are proposed targets, not completed or approved new images.

| Value | Proposed target and composition | Where the difficulty comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | Leonardo DiCaprio — the Gatsby toast already used in the invitation. | The glass, tuxedo and setting make a welcoming first picture. The existing recreation is usable now. |
| 200 | Gordon Ramsay — recognisable chef styling and kitchen setting, Sara's face. | Strong occupation cue; the facial expression is part of the joke. |
| 300 | Taylor Swift — a recognisable stage image, with the face replaced but useful styling retained. | Still familiar, with fewer setting clues. Avoid text or a visible tour logo that spells out the answer. |
| 400 | Rihanna — a carefully chosen portrait with recognisable styling. | The team needs the remaining facial/styling cues rather than a named brand or album cover. |
| 500 | Ryan Gosling — a close portrait with a stronger Sara blend and limited background. | A recognisable face remains, but there is less context. Reorder after a blind rehearsal if this is easier than another image. |

**Inputs needed:** confirm these five targets or send replacements; choose one or two clear Sara face references; pick or let us prepare an appropriate original celebrity reference for each target. Each completed clue needs the mashup, the exact original, the accepted celebrity name and any acceptable alternative spelling. “Name the actor” and “name the character” must never be interchangeable instructions.

The eight supplied memes are excellent **reactions**, but they do not establish which celebrity is supposedly underneath them. Keep them for correct/wrong reveals and host-triggered jokes. Use one quick reaction after a score decision, then restore the clue; do not cover the question while people are answering. The files themselves are static, so the game provides the movement.

## 3. What's That Country?

Keep the reference game's visual appeal. The current “Paris + Eiffel Tower = France” text questions are useful controls tests, but they are too uniform for a finished country column. Use a visual progression and accept English or Norwegian country names.

| Value | Format and specific proposed clue | Answer | Asset requirement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | A large, upright outline of Italy, including Sicily and Sardinia. “Which country?” | Italy / Italia. | Accurate outline on black, gold fill; no labels. |
| 200 | A recognisable crop of the Eiffel Tower without a city label. “Which country?” | France / Frankrike. | Permitted landmark photo; enough structure visible to identify it. |
| 300 | Outline of Japan with its main islands together, upright. “Which country?” | Japan. | Accurate island positions; do not scatter islands decoratively. |
| 400 | Iceland's outline, with no flag or capital name. “Which country?” | Iceland / Island. | Accurate outline. A “capital: Reykjavík” clue is held for an unscored rescue if the room is stuck. |
| 500 | A small regional map with New Zealand highlighted and labels removed. “Which country is highlighted?” | New Zealand. | Retain enough surrounding geography to reason it out; keep north up. |

Avoid flags made from emoji, which render differently across devices, and avoid rotating a familiar outline simply to make it unfair. Any map outline must come from an accurate geographic source; geographic accuracy and the actual visual difficulty need checking when the assets are prepared.

The host can replace **one** generic image with a Sara travel photo once its country is confirmed. Ask the country, not an obscure hotel or beach. The people who took the photo should not be the only team capable of answering it.

## 4. The Roaring Twenties

Mix a light dance question, a word, film technology, a book and a short chronology. Keep the setting explicit: US Prohibition and Norway's spirits ban were different rules in different places. The invitation's playful bootlegger licence is not a historical fact to teach or test.

| Value | Format and proposed clue | Accepted answer / reveal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | Three large choices: “Which dance belongs at a 1920s party: Charleston, Macarena or moonwalk?” | Charleston. An optional permitted dance clip can play only after the reveal. |
| 200 | A period-style bar sign with no answer printed on it: “During US Prohibition, what was an illicit bar commonly called?” | Speakeasy. One-sentence explanation on reveal. |
| 300 | Silent-film title card followed by a speech-bubble graphic: “Films found their voices. What English nickname did films with spoken dialogue acquire?” | Talkies. The question explicitly asks for the English term in both languages. |
| 400 | A recreated book-title graphic: “Who wrote the 1925 novel The Great Gatsby?” | F. Scott Fitzgerald; Fitzgerald is enough. The author's name must be absent from the clue artwork. |
| 500 | Three shuffled event cards: **The Great Gatsby published**, **Norwegian referendum on the spirits ban**, **Norwegian spirits ban formally repealed**. “Put them in chronological order.” | Novel: 1925 → referendum: 1926 → formal repeal: 1927. All three in order for 500 points. |

This upgrades the current 500-point question: simply remembering “1927” from the invitation is easy; distinguishing the vote from the legal change asks the team to connect the story. The chronology also makes a satisfying three-card reveal.

Historical sources: [Library of Congress on flappers](https://guides.loc.gov/chronicling-america-flappers), [The Mob Museum on speakeasies](https://prohibition.themobmuseum.org/the-history/the-prohibition-underworld/the-speakeasies-of-the-1920s/), [Library of Congress on sound cinema](https://loc.gov/loc/lcib/08012/silent.html), [Library of Congress on Gatsby](https://loc.gov/exhibits/america-reads/1900-to-1949.html) and [FHI's Norwegian alcohol-history overview](https://www.fhi.no/le/alkohol/alkoholinorge/alkohol-i-historien/historisk-oversikt-alkohol-i-norge-1816-2019/).

## 5. The Sara Archives

This is where the guest submissions become a game. Aim for affectionate discoveries, not a test of who has known Sara longest. The examples below are **formats awaiting real material**; bracketed text is not a claim about her life.

| Value | Format and specific question template | What the host needs to supply | Fairness for newer friends |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | Show the supplied action photo: “Which sport was Sara playing?” | Already confirmed by the user: table tennis/ping-pong. | The picture gives the answer; everyone can participate. |
| 200 | Show a travel photo with three country choices: “Where was this taken?” | One original photo, confirmed country, two plausible alternative countries. | Preserve scenery/context. Nobody needs to know the trip date. |
| 300 | “Finish Sara's real sentence: ‘[verified beginning] …’” Show three endings. | Exact genuine quote, who remembers it and the real ending. Two invented alternatives must be clearly treated as quiz distractors. | Include one sentence of context; a guest can infer tone without knowing the incident. |
| 400 | A short account stops at the critical moment: “[Verified situation]. What did Sara do next?” Three plausible endings. | The real story in 3–5 sentences, the confirmed ending, and permission to use it at the party. | Give enough information to reason. All options should be plausible; the true answer must not just be the longest. |
| 500 | An evidence board: three short, verified quotes and three named portraits — Sara plus two guests. “Match each quote to its speaker.” | Three exact quotes with confirmed speakers, three usable portraits and a brief relationship/context label for each person. | Include context such as “Sara's teammate” or “university friend.” No requirement to recognise an unfamiliar guest by face alone. |

The 500-point matching clue is one question with three parts. Use an announced all-correct rule for the full value, or decide fixed partial credit before the game; do not invent scoring rules after seeing a team's answer. Allow more reading time for this clue if the timer is used.

If the matching material is weak, replace it with **“Which photo came first?”** using three dated, host-verified photos and contextual clues. Do not guess ages, years, achievements or chronology from how Sara looks. The trophy photograph proves there is a trophy in the picture; it does not tell us which tournament she won.

## 6. Party Science

Short questions tied to things people can picture. Show a glass, a cold drink, a speaker or a spinning ball as the visual subject. The explanation is the payoff; no formula memorisation is needed.

| Value | Format and specific proposed clue | Accepted answer / explanation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | Close-up of sparkling water: “What gas makes the bubbles?” | Carbon dioxide / CO₂. Works just as well with a soft drink as champagne. |
| 200 | Ice in a glass of water: “The ice floats because it is more dense or less dense than liquid water?” | Less dense. Compare density, not the vague answer “it weighs less.” |
| 300 | Droplets on the outside of a cold glass: “Did this water leak through the glass, or come from the air?” | Water vapour from the air condensed on the cold surface. Accept condensation plus a plain explanation. |
| 400 | A light and a speaker separated from an observer by a vacuum: “Which signal can cross the gap: light, sound, both or neither?” | Light. Sound needs a material medium; light does not. Specify a vacuum, rather than claiming there can never be sound anywhere in space. |
| 500 | Diagram of a table-tennis ball with topspin moving through air: “What makes it curve down more than gravity alone: spin changing airflow, extra weight from rotation, or the table attracting it?” | Spin changes the airflow and creates an aerodynamic force. The name **Magnus effect** is a reveal fact, not a required password. |

The final clue brings the column back to Sara's table-tennis photos. It asks for a mechanism with clear options, so a non-physicist can participate. Use a correct diagram; spin direction and motion direction must agree.

Sources: [American Chemical Society on soda fizz](https://www.acs.org/education/whatischemistry/adventures-in-chemistry/secret-science-stuff/soda-pop.html), [USGS on water density](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/water-density), [USGS on condensation](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/condensation-and-water-cycle?page=1), [NASA on electromagnetic and mechanical waves](https://science.nasa.gov/ems/02_anatomy/) and [NASA Glenn on a spinning ball](https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/ideal-flow-around-spinning-ball/). These are proposed new clues; they are not in the running pack yet.

## Keeping the room involved

Build mixed teams where possible: someone who knows Sara's early years, someone from more recent years, and a newer friend. Every personal question should still offer visual evidence, context or choices. English and Norwegian should ask the same thing; accept answers in either language and sensible pronunciations of names.

The host selects the answering team, accepts an answer aloud, and applies plus or minus once. Allow a team to pass without a deduction; wrong attempts can lose the clue value. Decide the steal rule before starting, and reveal the answer only after all permitted attempts. The host-controlled game already supports the essential scoring workflow; this document does not introduce phone buzzers or guest logins.

Keep reactions brief, 2.6 seconds in the current build, with an immediate Continue button, with a reduced-motion alternative. Rotate Sara's correct/wrong stickers so the same face does not appear on every click. The joke should celebrate the room, not single out a guest. Let the host trigger an occasional meme manually without changing a score.

For timing, budget roughly 45–60 minutes including laughter and stories. That is a planning estimate, not a timer requirement. Avoid a long animation after each of 30 questions; spend the time on the answer reveals and real anecdotes.

## Optional finale: one last face

After the board, show **one extra celebrity/Sara mashup** in three progressively clearer stages. This is a shared final question, not a seventh column. Use a new celebrity so the answer has not appeared already.

For a simple version, every team writes one answer after the same final reveal; all correct teams receive 300 points, no deductions. If the standings are tied, use it only for tied teams. The three reveal stages are theatre rather than a speed advantage, so there is no need for buzzers or disputed shouts. A private final image and original reference are required; this finale is proposed, not implemented.

## Minimum preparation checklist

1. Confirm the six columns and whether Party Science or Bad Movie Plots takes the sixth position.
2. Confirm or replace the five celebrity mashup targets, and select the Sara face references.
3. Provide one confirmed travel photo, one real quote, one short story with its ending, and the three-speaker material for the final Sara question. The host can send rough notes; polished prose is not required.
4. Prepare the visual assets, neutral filenames and accepted answers; check every clue on the actual television.
5. Review the final private question pack. The public rehearsal pack exposes its answers in browser code, so surprise anecdotes and unrevealed final answers should not simply be committed into it.
